# Apparent depth

**Definition.** An object in one medium, viewed from directly above out of another medium, appears at depth $d' = d\,\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$. Here d is the real depth, $n_1$ the index of the object's medium and $n_2$ the index of the observer's medium (L3a slide 3).

## Explanation
Light from a coin in water bends away from the normal as it leaves into air. Your eye assumes light travels in straight lines, traces the rays back, and places the coin where those lines meet: higher than it really is. Looking from water up into air reverses the bending, so objects above the surface look farther away.

![[L3a-s03-apparent-depth.png]]
*Figure (L3a slide 3): (a) a slanted view; (b) the view from directly above.*

The formula holds only for viewing nearly straight down. Slide 3 notes that for a slanted view (its figure a) the apparent depth is hard to determine. The slides give the result without a derivation; it follows from Snell's law when the angles are small enough that $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta$ [Guessing: this small-angle step is not in the course materials].

## Worked detail: L3a-Ex4 recomputed
A swimmer treading water looks straight down at a coin on the bottom of a pool 3.00 m deep. The coin is in water ($n_1 = 1.33$) and the swimmer's eyes are in air ($n_2 = 1.00$):
$$d' = 3.00 \times \frac{1.00}{1.33} = 2.26\ \text{m}$$
This matches the slide.

## Recall
> [!question] Recall 1 · Calculation (L3a slide 3)
> A fish tank is filled with water to a depth of 0.40 m. How deep does it look from directly above?

> [!success]- Answer
> 0.40/1.33 = 0.30 m.

> [!question] Recall 2 · Prediction (L3a slide 3)
> A diver looks straight up at a seagull. Does the seagull look nearer or farther than it really is?

> [!success]- Answer
> Farther. Now $n_1 = 1.00$ (air, where the object is) and $n_2 = 1.33$ (water, where the observer is), so $d' = 1.33\,d$.
