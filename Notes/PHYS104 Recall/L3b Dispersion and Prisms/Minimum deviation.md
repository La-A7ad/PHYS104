# Minimum deviation

**Definition.** As the angle of incidence changes, a prism's deviation passes through a smallest value, $\delta_{min}$, reached when the ray passes symmetrically: $i_1 = i_2$ and $r_1 = r_2 = A/2$. Then $n = \sin\frac{A+\delta_{min}}{2}\big/\sin\frac{A}{2}$ (L3b slide 10).

## Explanation
Raising $i_1$ from its smallest allowed value, δ first falls, reaches $\delta_{min}$, then rises (slide 9). Paths are reversible, so swapping $i_1$ and $i_2$ gives the same δ; a single minimum must therefore have $i_1 = i_2$. Inside an isosceles prism the minimum-deviation ray runs parallel to the base.

From the symmetry, $r = A/2$ and $\delta_{min} = 2i - A$, so $i = (A + \delta_{min})/2$, and Snell's law $n = \sin i/\sin r$ gives the formula. The method is accurate because δ is flat near its minimum (slide 10). $\delta_{min}$ grows with n and with A, so it is larger for violet than for red (slide 11).

## Recall
> [!question] Recall 1 · Calculation (L3b slide 10)
> Find δmin for a 60.0° prism with n = 1.60.

> [!success]- Answer
> $\sin\frac{60^\circ + \delta}{2} = 1.60 \times 0.500 = 0.800$, so $\frac{60^\circ + \delta}{2} = 53.1^\circ$ and δmin = 46.3°.

> [!question] Recall 2 · Explanation (L3b slide 10)
> Why does setting up at minimum deviation make the measurement of n accurate?

> [!success]- Answer
> Near the minimum, δ hardly changes with the angle of incidence, so small errors in setting $i_1$ barely change the measured δmin.
